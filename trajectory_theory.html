Making the ball draw shapes

The theory behind trajectory_demo.py: how to turn a star or a figure 8 into plate tilts, and why the same PID that balances the ball is not enough on its own to follow a moving target.

Short version. Balancing holds the ball at one fixed point. Tracking moves that point along a shape. Plan a smooth reference position for every instant, work out the acceleration that reference needs, and tilt the plate to give the ball exactly that acceleration (feedforward). The PID then only corrects the small leftover error (feedback). In simulation this takes the circle from about 40 mm of error to about 4 mm.

1Path and trajectory are different things

A path is geometry only: the set of points that make up a star. A trajectory is a path plus a timetable: where the ball should be at every instant. The controller needs the trajectory, because the plate has to produce the right acceleration at the right time.

The same path can be feasible or impossible depending on the timetable. A 60 mm circle in 8 seconds needs about half a degree of tilt. The same circle in 2 seconds needs more than the plate can give. Choosing the timetable is called time scaling, and it is where most of the planning work is.

2The model: tilt is acceleration

A ball rolling without slipping on a plate tilted by a small angle θ accelerates at

a = g sin θ / (1 + I / m r2) For a hollow ball (ping-pong), I = ⅔ m r2, so a = 0.6 g sin θ, about 0.103 m/s² per degree.

Position is not controlled directly. Tilt sets acceleration, and the ball position is that acceleration integrated twice. The logs from this platform give a lower gain than the ideal value, plus a delay:

ẍ(t) = K · θ(t − τ) K = 0.070 m/s² per degree and τ = 0.20 s, measured by analyze_log.py. The same equation holds for y, with roll in place of pitch.

This one line is the whole plant model, and both the planner and the controller are built on it. Pitch moves the ball along x and roll moves it along y, so the two axes are planned and controlled separately.

3Describing the shapes

Smooth curves

A circle and a figure 8 are written as functions of an angle φ that grows steadily with time, φ = 2πt / T, where T is the lap time:

Circle:   x = R cos φ,   y = R sin φ
Figure 8:   x = R sin φ,   y = R sin φ cos φ

These are smooth everywhere, so their accelerations are finite everywhere. For the circle the acceleration has constant size R(2π/T)² and always points at the centre.

Shapes with corners

A star, square or triangle is a list of waypoints joined by straight lines. The five-pointed star is five points on a circle, visited by skipping one each time.

The ball cannot go round a sharp corner at speed: changing direction instantly needs infinite acceleration. So the ball must stop at each corner, and each straight segment becomes a rest-to-rest move.

4Time scaling: how to move along a line

For a segment from point A to point B taking time Ts, write the position as

p(t) = A + (B − A) · s(u),   u = t / Ts s runs from 0 to 1 and says what fraction of the segment has been covered.

The simplest choice, s = u (constant speed), asks the ball to jump from rest to full speed instantly. The demo uses the minimum-jerk polynomial instead:

s(u) = 10u3 − 15u4 + 6u5 Speed and acceleration are both zero at the start and at the end.
Minimum jerk Constant speed

Constant speed needs an infinite spike of acceleration at each end (arrows). Minimum jerk starts and ends at zero acceleration, so the plate tilt starts and ends level.

For a segment of length L the peaks are:

vmax = 1.875 L / Ts     amax = 5.77 L / Ts2

Acceleration falls with the square of the time. Doubling the segment time needs a quarter of the tilt.

The same polynomial is used twice more in the demo: to glide the ball from wherever it is to the start of the shape when you press SPACE, and (as a speed ramp) to start the circle and figure 8 gently, since those begin at full speed.

5Is the trajectory feasible?

Because tilt is acceleration, the plate's tilt limit is an acceleration limit:

aref ≤ K · θbudget The demo gives feedforward 60% of the 5° limit (3°, or 0.21 m/s²) and keeps the rest for feedback. It refuses to run a shape that needs more and suggests a slower lap time.

The tilt limit is not the only limit. The 0.2 s dead time and the error in K both matter more as the lap gets faster, so the defaults sit well inside the tilt budget.

6Why PID alone lags behind

The balancing controller computes tilt from the error only:

θ = kp e + ki ∫e dt + kd ė,    e = ref − ball

To follow a circle the plate must be tilted about half a degree at all times. A feedback-only controller can only produce tilt when there is error, so it has to keep an error to keep the tilt. At kp = 20° per metre, half a degree costs roughly 25 mm of error, and the dead time makes it worse. The ball traces a shrunken, rotated copy of the shape.

Raising the gains would shrink that error, but the dead time caps the usable gain. That is the instability CHANGES.md describes.

7Feedforward: use what the plan already knows

The reference is known in advance, so its acceleration is known in advance. Invert the model from section 2 to get the tilt that produces it:

θff(t) = r̈(t + τ) / K r is the reference position. Evaluating it τ = 0.2 s ahead means the tilt takes effect when the reference needs it, which cancels most of the dead time.

The total command is feedforward plus feedback:

θ = θff + PID(r − x)

This arrangement is called two-degree-of-freedom control. Each part has its own job:

  • Feedforward supplies the tilt the shape needs. It acts with no error and no delay, but it is only as good as the model.
  • Feedback corrects what the model gets wrong: the error in K, the plate being off level (the integral term), an uneven ball, a nudge from outside.

If the model were perfect, the error would stay at zero and the PID would do nothing. With K wrong by 10%, about 10% of the feedback-only error is left.

This is also why sections 3 to 5 insist on smoothness. Feedforward uses the second derivative of the reference. A corner taken at speed has an infinite second derivative, which is a tilt the plate cannot produce.

The formal name. A system is differentially flat when every input can be computed from a chosen output and its derivatives. Here the ball position is that output: given a twice-differentiable reference, the tilt follows from one division. Robot arms and quadrotors are planned the same way, with more derivatives.

Things to try

  1. Feedforward off. The error jumps by roughly ten times and the ball's trail becomes a smaller, rotated copy of the shape.
  2. Look-ahead off. Feedforward still helps, but the ball runs about 0.2 s late.
  3. Lap time down to 4 s. The peak feedforward tilt grows with 1/T² and the error grows with it. Below the feasible limit the readout turns red.
  4. Error in K to 0%. With a perfect model the remaining error is small. At ±40% feedforward still beats feedback alone.
  5. Star against circle. The star stops at each point, where the error is smallest. The error peaks mid-segment, where acceleration is highest.

Run python analyze_log.py on a recent balancing log to re-measure the gain and the dead time, then pass them as --plant-gain and --delay.

What has been tested. The demo has been run against the simulated ball only, where it tracks every shape to about 3 to 5 mm RMS. It has not been run on the real platform. The real ball's uneven weight will add error at the points of the star, squares and triangles, where the ball stops; the circle and figure 8 keep it rolling and should suffer least.
